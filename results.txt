By Simply Psychology Editorial January 8, 2026 min read AI-assisted draft · reviewed by our editorial team At some point in their 30s or 40s, many people look around and realize: making new friends has become inexplicably hard. The easy camaraderie of school years — built on daily proximity and unstructured time — has given way to a social world organized around work and family, where new friendships feel effortful, awkward, and somehow slightly embarrassing to pursue. This experience is not weakness; it is the predictable outcome of well-studied social and developmental processes. And understanding those processes is the first step toward building the social life — and the mental health — that genuine friendship provides. Why Childhood Friendship Formation Is Easier Several structural features of childhood make friendship formation relatively effortless: Propinquity : Being physically close to the same people repeatedly is the foundational condition for friendship. Schools place children in daily contact with large numbers of age-mates for extended periods. Unstructured time : The hours of recess, lunch, and unscheduled time in childhood allow casual interaction — the "hanging out" that builds friendship through accumulated low-stakes shared experience. Developmental mandate : Children are at life stages where peer connection is developmentally central. Making friends is what you're supposed to be doing. Low psychological stakes : Children typically have fewer filters, less self-consciousness, and less fear of rejection. The social cost of a friendship overture is relatively low. Adult life inverts almost all of these conditions. What Makes Adult Friendship Formation Difficult Reduced Proximity Adults organize their lives around home, work, and family — not peer community. Unless you happen to live near potential friends (increasingly uncommon in a mobile society) or work in environments with genuine social opportunity, the structural conditions for friendship just aren't present. You can go weeks without sustained contact with anyone outside your domestic and professional environments. In the context of adult life — with full-time work, family responsibilities, commutes, and domestic tasks — accumulating 200 hours with a new person is a substantial challenge. Existing Network Inertia Adults who have existing friendships, however attenuated, face less acute motivation to form new ones than someone who is entirely socially isolated. The "good enough" existing network can suppress the energy needed to form genuinely new friendships. The Mental Health Stakes of Adult Friendship Why does this matter beyond social comfort? The research on friendship and health is striking. Loneliness as a Health Threat Loneliness has been described as a public health epidemic. Research by John Cacioppo showed that chronic loneliness activates a hypervigilant threat-detection state in the brain, increases inflammatory markers, disrupts sleep, and elevates blood pressure. Subjective loneliness predicts all-cause mortality, cardiovascular disease, dementia, and depression. A meta-analysis by Holt-Lunstad, Smith, and Layton synthesizing data from over 300,000 people found that adequate social relationships were associated with a 50% increased likelihood of survival — an effect size comparable to quitting smoking and exceeding obesity, physical inactivity, and hypertension as a mortality predictor. This is not hyperbole. Social connection is one of the most powerful health variables we have. Friendship Quality vs. Quantity Research consistently shows that it's the quality rather than quantity of friendships that drives mental health outcomes. A 2022 study in Social Psychological and Personality Science found that having even one genuinely close, emotionally reciprocal friendship significantly buffered against depression and anxiety. People with many superficial connections but no deep friendships showed similar vulnerability to those with limited social contact. This finding is practically important: the goal is not to maximize the number of friends but to invest in depth with a smaller number of relationships. Propinquity and Repeated Exposure The most reliably replicated finding in friendship research is the propinquity effect: we become friends with people we're physically near. This works primarily through repeated unplanned encounters — seeing the same person at a recurring event, class, or community activity builds familiarity, and familiarity breeds liking (the "mere exposure effect" documented by Robert Zajonc). The practical implication is powerful: consistency and regularity of exposure matters more than the specific context. Joining a recurring activity — a running club, a book group, a class, a choir, a sports league — creates the propinquity conditions that make friendship formation more likely. The activity itself is almost secondary; what matters is that it brings you into repeated proximity with the same people. Self-Disclosure and Reciprocity Research by Arthur and Elaine Aron on "fast friends" procedures shows that gradually escalating self-disclosure — sharing increasingly personal information and feelings reciprocally — accelerates friendship formation significantly. The famous "36 Questions" experiment demonstrated that two strangers who engaged in this kind of escalating disclosure reported stronger feelings of connection than pairs who had conventional conversations. In practical terms, this means being willing to share something personally meaningful earlier in a new relationship than social convention might suggest — and responding with reciprocal openness when others do the same. Proactive Initiative Research documents what Hall and colleagues call the "friendship formation burden" — the need for someone to take initiative. Most adult friendships require someone to take the first step, and research shows that people consistently underestimate how positively their social overtures will be received (an effect called "reachout effect"). The discomfort of inviting a new acquaintance to coffee or a social event is typically much greater in the anticipation than in the reality. Presence and Attention In existing relationships, research shows that the single most powerful friendship maintenance behavior is attentiveness — giving genuine, focused attention when together. In an era of constant digital distraction, being the person who is fully present with someone is increasingly distinctive and powerful. Maintaining Adult Friendships Forming friendships is only half the challenge. Maintaining them through the compressed schedules and life transitions of adulthood requires deliberate effort. Research by William Rawlins identifies freedom, trust, and mutual investment as the three pillars of adult friendship maintenance.

Why is similarity in conscientiousness so strongly related to group success? The ultimate reason may relate to the crucial role of timing and coordination in human sociality. Since human groups typically disperse in daytime and come together in the evenings, following a fission-fusion pattern, our ancestors faced the challenge of how to maintain contact and coordination with physically absent others ( Pearce, 2014 ). From this perspective, the tendency to bond with others with similar conscientiousness levels may reflect the challenge of group coordination: when to meet, and how to fulfill certain collective planning and action tasks ( Berg, 2016 Dunbar and Shultz, 2017 ). Considering the male-hunting-group hypothesis (e.g., DeVore and Tooby, 1987 ), this seems to make sense, since similar orientation to group work ethic is tantamount to the success of the group. Primate research has investigated why males are more gregarious compared to females (e.g., Wrangham, 2000 ), and the bigger benefits of group formation males appear to acquire. Discussion Do pre-existing neural similarities predict future friendship? The current results provide evidence for neural homophily, such that relative to people who ended up far from one another in social ties, people who ended up becoming friends with one another demonstrated greater pre-existing neural similarity before meeting one another. Additionally, pre-existing neural similarity was particularly strongly linked to changes in inter-individual social distance between two and eight months after entering a new community. Such changes reflected the formation, persistence and dissolution of friendships in the community’s social network. Relative to individuals who had a social distance of 3 after having lived in a new community for eight months, individuals who became friends exhibited greater pre-existing neural similarity in a portion of the left OFC. Given the role of the OFC in processing subjective value , neural similarity in this brain region may reflect similarities in tastes and preferences (for example, similarities in what individuals find funny or otherwise appealing), which may become aligned across certain individuals as a function of sociodemographic similarities. Inter-individual similarity in sociodemographic variables (that is, age, gender, nationality, hometown size and location, undergraduate alma mater, undergraduate major, and industry) appeared to drive the differences in pre-existing neural similarity in the OFC that were observed between friends and friends-of-friends-of-friends. In particular, accounting for gender similarity significantly reduced differences in pre-existing neural similarity between friends and distance 3 dyads in this brain region. However, controlling for inter-individual similarity in the extent to which individuals were interested in the stimuli did not significantly diminish the difference in pre-existing neural similarity between friends and individuals characterized by a social distance of 3. Although neural similarity in this brain region may reflect similarity in the processing of subjective value, inter-individual similarity in self-reported ratings of preferences may not completely account for the subjective value processing that may be particularly aligned among friends.
